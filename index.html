About

Most of what I can do, I learned by taking on work I wasn’t yet qualified for

I build analytical methods and the instruments that run them. What I care about is the moment a measurement becomes trustworthy — when a number stops being a reading and starts being evidence somebody can act on.

I began as a laboratory technologist in Ghana, building electronic instrumentation and running teaching laboratories at the University of Cape Coast. That work was hands-on and unglamorous: calibration schedules, preventive maintenance, circuit boards that had to be designed because no one was going to buy us one. It taught me that instruments are not black boxes. Somebody built them, and somebody has to understand them well enough to know when they are lying to you.

Analytical chemistry pulled me in from there. My published work came out of multi-institution collaborations across chromatography and mass spectrometry — determining extractables from rubber closures to assess drug–container compatibility, developing high-throughput lipid analysis by supercritical fluid chromatography, screening food-contact materials for compounds nobody had thought to look for. Eleven papers, and in every one of them my contribution was a component of something larger that other people were leading.

Then I moved to Georgia Tech, to a different country and a different scientific culture, and was handed a problem with no established solution: build a microfluidic system that generates aqueous droplets in a gas phase, under heat, stably enough to do chemistry inside them. No template existed. I learned fluid dynamics, fabrication, and high-speed imaging analysis largely from scratch, and taught myself Python to make sense of the data the system produced. The first several designs failed. That stretch was uncomfortable in a way coursework never is, and it is the single most useful thing that has happened to my training — it is where I learned to diagnose an unfamiliar system and stay with a problem until it gives.

Alongside the research I founded the Analytical Instrumentation Training Program in my department, after noticing that graduate students were leaving with theoretical knowledge and thin hands-on instrument experience. Building it meant persuading faculty, designing curriculum around a real gap, and changing the program repeatedly as we found out what actually worked. I have also authored and revised standard operating procedures and carried responsibility for safety in teaching labs running more than a hundred students a year. Documentation and safety are not overhead to me. They are what make a result defensible and a laboratory a place people can depend on.

I am now looking for work in industry — analytical development, R&D, applications, instrumentation, or process development. I am not looking to carry my own research agenda into a company. I want to point what I can do at problems a team is already working on, and I am comfortable being the newest person in the room while I learn how they do it.
